Ledger keeps your hours in a record only you control, shows what the agreement actually pays for the day you worked, and reads the public federal filings your local and your pension fund are required to publish.

It is free.

No account, no subscription, no ads, no trial, no upgrade later. It was built by a laborer for the guys he works with.

What it does

Logs a day in twenty seconds

Punch in and out, or just type the hours. Overtime splits itself using the real rules in the agreement — not jobsite folklore.

Knows what a short day pays

Rained out at ten? Sent home at the gate? Article 39 sets a floor, and the app tells you what it is and cites the paragraph.

Watches your cards

OSHA, HAZWOPER, forklift, CPR. Warnings at 90, 30 and 7 days — enough time to actually book the refresher.

Reads the filings for you

What the officers are paid, where the money goes, how funded your pension really is, and which contractors the funds are suing.

Common questions

Where are my hours stored?

On your phone, and nowhere else. There is no server. Nothing you log is uploaded, and no one — including me — can read it. See the privacy policy.

What happens if I lose my phone?

Your log goes with it. That is the trade for having nobody else hold your records. Use Settings → Back up. It writes two files: one you can read and print, and one that restores everything. Send them to yourself and keep them somewhere you will still have in a year. Do this before you need it, not after.

Can I change an entry after I save it?

You can void it, and you can log a correction. You cannot silently rewrite it. Every entry is sealed with a hash that links to the one before it, so the log can show it has not been tampered with. A crossed-out line in a logbook is still evidence; a log that can be quietly edited is not.

The overtime split looks wrong for my day.

The app uses the rules in the published agreement, which sometimes differ from how a job actually pays. Tap How this day is paid under the hours to see the rule and the article it comes from. If your job pays something different, that is worth a conversation with your steward — and email me if you think the app has the rule wrong.

Why does it ask why my day was short?

Because showing up is paid time. Article 39 sets a floor for a day cut short by weather, by being sent home, or by waiting on the site, and most people have never read it. The app tells you the floor and cites the paragraph. It never changes your hours by itself.

The microphone will not work.

Open the iPhone Settings app, scroll down to Ledger, and turn on Microphone and Speech Recognition. Dictation runs on the device itself.

Are the salary and pension numbers real?

They come from filings that unions and benefit plans are required by federal law to make public — LM-2 reports to the U.S. Department of Labor and Form 5500 reports under ERISA. The app shows what is in those filings and says which one. It does not editorialize the numbers, and anything it deduces rather than reads is labeled as a deduction.

What does the guy who built it get out of it?

Nothing, and the design is how you can check that. There is no price, no ads, no upgrade, no account — and no server, so there is no data to collect, sell, or trade even if someone wanted to. A laborer built it because he wanted it for himself and the guys he works with. The only thing it costs him is the time to maintain it.

My company already gives us an app for hours.

Keep using it — and ask yourself whose record it is. The company's app writes to the company's system, and when a check comes up short, the argument is about numbers they keep. Ledger is your record, on your phone, sealed so it can show it was never edited. Under U.S. wage law, when an employer's records are inadequate, a worker's own contemporaneous records carry real weight. Two minutes a day buys you your own copy of the truth.

Is this the union's app?

No. Ledger is published independently by The Undesirables LLC. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, sponsored by or connected to LIUNA, any local union, district council, benefit fund, or employer. Union names appear only as they appear in the public records the app cites.

What this app is not

It is not legal or financial advice. It shows public records and the plain terms of published agreements so you can go ask better questions. Verify anything that matters with your local, your fund office, or a lawyer.

Lawsuits shown on the Radar tab are filed claims, taken from public federal dockets. A filed suit is an allegation until a court says otherwise, and every entry links to the record so you can read it yourself.
