Ledger keeps your hours in a record only you control, shows what the agreement actually pays for the day you worked, and reads the public federal filings your local and your pension fund are required to publish.

It is free.

No account, no subscription, no ads, no trial, no upgrade later. It was built by a laborer for the guys he works with.

What it does

Logs a day in twenty seconds

Punch in and out, or just type the hours. Overtime splits itself using the real rules in the agreement — not jobsite folklore.

Knows what a short day may be owed

Rained out at ten? Sent home at the gate? The app tells you what the agreement may owe you and cites the paragraph. Most of Article 39 is written for Will and Grundy counties. Work anywhere else? Ask your steward before you use that floor.

Watches your cards

OSHA, HAZWOPER, forklift, CPR. Each card counts down on the Cards tab. From version 1.0.1, inside 30 days or once it lapses, a line also shows on the Log tab — the screen you open every day. There are no phone notifications.

Reads the filings for you

What the officers are paid, where the money goes, how funded your pension really is, and which contractors the funds are suing.

Common questions

Where are my hours stored?

On your phone. There is no server: Ledger uploads nothing you log, and I never see it. If your iPhone backs up to iCloud or a computer, that backup may include Ledger's data, the same as any app's. On the web version your hours are in that browser's storage, and clearing the browser's data erases them. The browser can also clear them on its own: on iPhone, Safari may erase them if you don't open the page for about a week, unless you've added Ledger to your Home Screen. Don't count on the phone to keep them — save a backup. See the privacy policy.

What happens if I lose my phone?

Your log may go with it. That is the trade for having nobody else hold your records. Restoring your iPhone's own iCloud or computer backup might bring it back, but don't count on it. Use Settings → Save a backup. It writes two files: one you can read and print, and one that restores your work log. Your cards, paydays and settings are not in the backup yet, so write your card numbers down too. On iPhone it opens the share sheet. Save the files to iCloud Drive (not On My iPhone), AirDrop them to another phone or computer, or mail them to a personal address — a copy that stays on this phone goes with it. Keep them somewhere you will still have in a year. Do this before you need it, not after.

Can I change an entry after I save it?

You can remove it with its × in the Recent list on the Log tab (your latest 30 entries) and log it again. It stays in the record marked as removed. You cannot silently rewrite it. Every entry is sealed with a hash that links to the one written before it, so an entry changed afterwards no longer matches its seal, and the backup report says whether the chain is intact. It is not a notary: the seal time comes from your phone's clock, and someone who knows how the seal works could build a whole new log from scratch. A crossed-out line in a logbook is still evidence; a log that can be quietly edited is not.

The overtime split looks wrong for my day.

The app uses the rules in the published agreement, which sometimes differ from how a job actually pays. Tap How this day is paid under the hours to see the rule and the article it comes from. Picked Four 10s? Straight time to 10 hours is allowed only in a week with a holiday (Art. 15 ¶1), so in any other week hours 9 and 10 split as overtime. Version 1.0 counted them as straight time — update the app. If your job pays something different, that is worth a conversation with your steward — and email me if you think the app has the rule wrong.

The date or time on my log looks off.

From version 1.0.1, Ledger keeps your log on Central (Chicago) time, whatever your phone is set to. Working in another time zone? Pick yours in Settings → Time zone. Don't see it? Update the app. Logged in the evening on version 1.0? That day may carry the next day's date. If it is in your Recent list, remove it with its × and log it again.

Why does it ask why my day was short?

Because showing up can be paid time. Article 39 sets a floor for a day cut short by weather, by being sent home, or by waiting on the site, and most people have never read it. Most of it is written for Will and Grundy counties, and the app can't tell which county you work in. So from version 1.0.1 it says what may be owed, cites the paragraph, and tells you to check with your steward. It never changes your hours by itself — you tap Use if you want the floor. If you don't work in Will or Grundy county, don't tap Use unless your steward says that floor applies to you. Outside Will and Grundy, building work goes by Art. 19, road work by Art. 26, and sewer or tunnel work by Art. 32, and ¶5's 2 hours of show-up time applies everywhere when weather stops the day and nobody called you off. The union has 45 days to file a grievance (Art. 11 ¶2), so tell your steward right away. Don't wait for the check.

The microphone will not work.

Open the iPhone Settings app, scroll down to Ledger, and turn on Microphone and Speech Recognition. From version 1.0.1, dictation runs only on the phone itself, and the web version has no mic. If your iPhone can't turn speech into text on the phone for your language, Ledger won't record — type the note instead.

Are the salary and pension numbers real?

They come from filings that unions and benefit plans are required by federal law to make public — LM-2 reports to the U.S. Department of Labor and Form 5500 reports under ERISA. The app shows what is in those filings and says which one. Where the app comments on a number, the comment is in its own words beside it, not part of the filing. The filings give no ID for a person, so one person's pay is matched across filings by name, and the app says so. A figure worked out from others shows its inputs.

What does the guy who built it get out of it?

Nothing, and the design is how you can check that. There is no price, no ads, no upgrade, no account — and no server, so there is no data to collect, sell, or trade even if someone wanted to. A laborer built it because he wanted it for himself and the guys he works with. The only thing it costs him is the time to maintain it.

My company already gives us an app for hours.

Keep using it — and ask yourself whose record it is. The company's app writes to the company's system, and when a check comes up short, the argument is about numbers they keep. Ledger is your record, on your phone, sealed so an entry changed later no longer matches. Under U.S. wage law, when an employer's records are inadequate, a worker's own contemporaneous records carry real weight. Two minutes a day buys you your own copy of the truth.

No. Ledger is published independently by The Undesirables LLC. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, sponsored by or connected to LIUNA, any local union, district council, benefit fund, or employer. Union names appear only as they appear in the public records the app cites.

What this app is not

It is not legal or financial advice. It shows public records and the plain terms of published agreements so you can go ask better questions. Verify anything that matters with your local, your fund office, or a lawyer.

Lawsuits shown on the Radar tab are filed claims, taken from public federal dockets. A filed suit is an allegation until a court says otherwise, and every entry links to the record so you can read it yourself. The list only runs to its last refresh, so newer suits are not on it; from version 1.0.1 it shows that date. When it flags a contractor in your log, that is a match on the name — open the docket to check it is the same company.
